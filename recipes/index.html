The Science of Food & Healing

Three eating styles, three different mechanisms, all with real clinical evidence behind them for diabetes management. Pick a style below to see the actual research -- the mechanism, the trial data, and an honest caveat -- then the recipes to put it into practice. 142 recipes total, full nutrition breakdown and glycemic index estimate on every one.

The Evidence — Keto & Low-Carb

Less dietary carbohydrate, less insulin demand

Cutting carbohydrate intake directly lowers the glucose load your body has to manage after every meal — which means less insulin has to be produced to handle it. For someone whose insulin response is already struggling, that's less demand on an already-overworked system. The number that actually matters here is net carbs (total carbs minus fiber), since fiber isn't broken down into glucose the way starches and sugars are — a recipe can show a higher gross-carb number and still be genuinely keto-appropriate if most of that number is fiber.

−0.29%
Average HbA1c reduction across 27 RCTs (2,870 people), largest effect at 3 months
−7.1 mg/dL−0.4 mmol/L

Some trials saw reduced need for diabetes medication
Honest caveat: benefits are real but front-loaded — effect size shrinks in most trials past the 3–6 month mark, and cholesterol response is inconsistent study to study. Worth monitoring lipids with your doctor if you go this route long-term.
Sources: Very Low Carbohydrate Diets in T2D, meta-analysis · Ketogenic Diet in T2D Prevention, narrative review · Low-Carb Diets in T2D, Eastern vs. Western populations
The Evidence — Vegan & Whole-Food Plant-Based

Less fat trapped inside your liver and muscle cells

Insulin resistance isn't just about how much sugar you eat — it's heavily driven by fat that accumulates inside liver and muscle cells (not the fat you can pinch), which physically interferes with insulin's signaling. Whole-food plant-based diets measurably clear that intracellular fat, and separately improve how well pancreatic beta cells actually secrete insulin — attacking both core defects of type 2 diabetes at once, through a different mechanism than carb restriction.

−0.36%
Pooled HbA1c reduction, 9 RCTs / 681 people (2024 meta-analysis) — above the FDA's own 0.3% bar for a new diabetes drug
−34% / −10%
Liver fat / muscle fat reduction in a 16-week low-fat vegan diet RCT
Vegan > Vegetarian
Subgroup analysis found vegan outperforms vegetarian specifically on cardiometabolic health
Honest caveat: the benefit shows up on a whole-food framing — none of these trials are testing vegan junk food — and most studies needed 12+ weeks to show a measurable effect, so this isn't a fast fix.
Sources: Vegetarian/Vegan Diets and Glycemic Health in T2D, Nutrition Reviews · Vegetarian/Vegan Patterns to Treat T2D, RCT meta-analysis · Plant-Based Diets and Insulin Sensitivity, meta-analysis
The Evidence — Mediterranean

Olive oil compounds that repair your cells' power plants

Extra virgin olive oil's polyphenols (hydroxytyrosol, oleuropein) activate a cellular pathway — SIRT1 → PGC-1α — that drives your cells to build more, healthier mitochondria and reduces oxidative damage to the ones you have. That matters for diabetes specifically because mitochondrial dysfunction is now seen as a unifying cause behind both core defects of type 2 diabetes: it directly impairs muscle glucose uptake, and it blunts insulin secretion in pancreatic beta cells, which is itself a mitochondria-driven process.

10% vs 18%
PREDIMED trial: diabetes incidence over ~4 years, olive-oil/nut-enriched Mediterranean diet vs. control
−0.3 to −0.5%
Typical HbA1c reduction in RCT meta-analyses of people with existing T2D
↓ FPG, BMI, LDL, BP
Consistently reduced alongside glycemic improvement across pooled trial data
Honest caveat: at least one recent meta-analysis found no significant change in fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, or HDL specifically — the HbA1c and diabetes-incidence effects are the well-replicated ones.
Sources: EVOO Phenolic Compounds & Mitochondrial Function · Mediterranean Diet in T2D Management (incl. PREDIMED) · Mediterranean Diet and Glycemic Control, RCT meta-analysis
